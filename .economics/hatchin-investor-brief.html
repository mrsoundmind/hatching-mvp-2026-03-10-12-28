Hatchin: what it costs, what it earns, and why it works

Hatchin gives anyone a team of 30 AI colleagues that live inside their project, chat with them, and do real work in the background. This brief explains the business behind it in simple terms, and shows why the numbers are strong.

Please read this first. Hatchin is a pre-revenue product. Everything here is a financial model and a plan, built on stated assumptions. These are projections, not results we have already achieved. Near the end we mark, honestly, which numbers are hard facts and which are estimates.

The business in one paragraph

Hatchin is cheap to run, keeps most of every rupee it earns, and gets more valuable the more a customer uses it. It costs about $3.22 (₹277) a month to serve a paying customer, who pays $19 (₹1,634) or more. Selling to a company is worth about 23 times a single user. The market is large, the cost to break even is tiny, and even a cautious version of the plan makes money. The main job is not saving cash, it is winning customers, especially companies.

Why the margins are unusually strong

the part your CTO will care about

The reason Hatchin can keep about 80% of every sale comes down to one technical choice. Chatting with the AI is almost free to run. Only the background work costs real money, and we cap it.

Chatting is nearly free

  • One chat message costs about $0.0004 (₹0.03). Simple messages run on Meta's open-source Llama (free); the rest on DeepSeek Flash, the cheapest paid API, with the prompt cached (about 98% cheaper).
  • Even 900 messages a month is just $0.45 (₹39). So we can let people chat as much as they like.
  • In plain terms: a million tokens of AI is about 750,000 words or 300 messages, and $1 (₹86) of AI covers roughly 2,000 messages.
  • A single background task costs about $0.009 (₹0.77) on average (a quick answer is less, a full document more), and people run many. This is metered as a token budget, so cost stays capped whatever the task.

Background work is capped, like Claude

  • Each user gets a budget of background tasks that refills every 5 hours, with a weekly ceiling. Same idea as Claude's usage limits.
  • This means no single user can ever run up a surprise bill. The most any user can cost is fixed in advance.
  • It is also the safety net that lets us price low in India and offer a free trial without losing money.

The one risk here worth naming to your CTO: the cheap AI comes mainly from a single provider on promotional pricing. We keep Google and open-source Llama ready as backups, and the whole model is built so we can switch and re-price quickly if that changes.

The growth plan

one realistic path over 18 months

This is a plan, not a promise, since Hatchin is pre-revenue. It shows how monthly income could build if the plan works. The good news is that the business covers its own running cost almost immediately, then grows from there.

Monthly income (MRR) building over 18 months

By quarter 6 that is a $129,000 (₹1.1 crore) a year run rate, and it is still climbing. The business covers its basic running cost (about $227, or ₹19,500, a month at that scale) by quarter 2. A few larger companies, instead of the small ones assumed here, would push this up further.

Why we believe these numbers

  • 10,000 signups in 18 months is about 550 a month, small at first then growing. The plan is a public launch (Product Hunt, Hacker News, founder communities) for the first few hundred, then referrals, an affiliate program, and partnerships with tools founders already use. We avoid paid ads because they lose money at this price. This is the plan's biggest assumption, and the main thing to prove.
  • 3 in 100 free users pay. A deliberately cautious figure. The usual for this kind of tool is nearer 4.5 in 100.
  • About 20 companies, roughly one a month, landed through startup programs, accelerators, and free users bringing their own teams.

How confident we are, honestly

  • Once the signups and paying customers exist, the income is simple arithmetic (their number times the price). That part is solid.
  • The signup numbers are the estimate, not a certainty. They depend on the plan to reach people actually working.
  • If signups come slower, the shape stays the same, it just arrives later. Nothing about the economics breaks; the timeline stretches.

Companies are the real engine

and they grow over time
23×

One company is worth as much as 23 solo users

Companies spend company money, use the product more, and stay far longer. A 10 person team is worth about $9,600 (₹8.3L) over its life, a 50 person team about $45,000 (₹39L). Better still, teams grow: they add seats over time, so the same customer pays more each year without us spending anything to win them again.

This is the pattern investors look for in software: land a small team, then grow inside it. It means revenue can grow even if we never add a single new company, and it keeps the cost of winning customers low.

Costs as we grow, and the path to profit

being straight about the future

Costs today, and later

  • Today, at launch scale: about $170 (₹14,620) a month for servers and tools. The founder takes no salary, so cash lasts a very long time.
  • Infrastructure scales with users, not flat: about $850 (₹73,100) a month at 10,000 signups and $4,400 (₹3.78L) at 100,000, as compute, database and email step up. It grows sub-linearly, so it stays well under 10% of revenue.
  • Team is the bigger, separate cost: as we hire, monthly cost rises to perhaps $10,000 to $20,000 (₹8.6L to ₹17L) for a small team. Because we keep about 80% of every sale, income still grows faster, roughly 15 to 30 companies cover a small paid team.

The path to profit

  • The business covers its basic costs almost at once (about 1 company, or 12 solo users).
  • It can grow to a $129,000 (₹1.1 crore) run rate in 18 months on the base plan, and keep going.
  • We are not raising money right now. This brief is to align the team and to help investors get to know the business early.

What is next, and why it does not break the margin

a future milestone, not built yet

The next wave of features is planned, not live: a coding agent (an engineer Hatch that writes real code, in the spirit of Claude), image generation, audio (voice and narration), and richer PDF and document export. Some of these are genuinely expensive to run. The principle we hold to is simple: cheap features stay in the base plan, expensive ones are metered and capped, so no feature can ever cost a user more than they pay.

Planned featureWhat it actually costsRoughly, per useHow we price it
PDF and document exportServer work, not an AI call. The writing is already counted.a fraction of a centFree, in the base plan
Image generationA per image API call (Flux, SDXL, Imagen)$0.02 to $0.08 ₹2 to ₹7A monthly image allowance, then per image
Audio (voice, narration)Per minute of text to speech or transcription$0.02 to $0.10 ₹2 to ₹9 a minuteA per minute cap, with a weekly ceiling
Coding agentPremium models, long multi step runs, plus a safe sandbox to run code$0.50 to $5 or more ₹43 to ₹430+ a taskMetered credits or a higher Builder tier, not the flat plan

How capping keeps the margin

  • Every expensive feature draws from a budget that refills weekly, the same idea as today's task cap. The most a user can cost is fixed in advance, so a heavy coding user can never run up a bill that beats their price.
  • Cheap features stay unlimited feeling in the base plan (chat, PDF). Expensive ones are add ons or a higher tier (coding, image, audio).
  • Net effect: new features grow revenue and usefulness without touching the 80% margin, because the cost is always capped below the price.

The one that changes the shape

  • The coding agent is the outlier: a single task can cost 50 to 500 times a normal background task, because it uses premium models and runs for many steps.
  • It also adds real infrastructure (a sandbox to run code safely), which lifts the fixed monthly bill when it ships.
  • So it is priced as usage, not unlimited. That keeps it a profit centre instead of a leak.

These are planning estimates for features that are not built yet. The exact caps and prices are set when each one ships, once we can measure real usage. The point of showing them now is that the pricing model already has a clear, safe answer for expensive features: meter them and cap them.

Sources and method

where the hard numbers come from

The cost side rests on published, checkable prices, current as of July 2026. The behaviour side (conversion, churn) uses industry benchmarks until Hatchin's own data replaces them. Every figure traces to one of these.

  1. AI model prices. DeepSeek API pricing, V4-Flash at $0.14 and $0.28 per million tokens in and out (api-docs.deepseek.com/quick_start/pricing); Google Gemini pricing (ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing). These set the per-message and per-task cost.
  2. The free tier for simple messages. Groq's hosted Llama 3.3-70B, an open Meta model, run at no per-token charge (console.groq.com). It is booked at $0 in our cost table, which is what makes chatting close to free.
  3. Infrastructure. Supabase Pro at $25 a month (supabase.com/pricing); Fly.io compute (fly.io/docs/about/pricing); Sentry, PostHog, uptime and email tools at their published list prices.
  4. Payment fees. Stripe at 2.9% plus $0.30 per charge (stripe.com/pricing); Razorpay for India (razorpay.com/pricing).
  5. Taxes and exchange rate. India GST 18% and corporate tax about 25% (CBIC and the Income Tax Department); UK VAT 20% (gov.uk/vat-rates); rupees converted at 86 to the dollar, the market rate in July 2026.
  6. Conversion and churn. We model free-to-paid at 3%, deliberately below the roughly 4 to 5% SaaS median; B2C monthly churn at 6%, higher in India on card-renewal friction. These are industry averages, not Hatchin data yet, and are flagged as estimates above.
  7. Our own measurements. Token usage per message, the prompt cache-hit rate, and the provider mix are read from Hatchin's own code and logs. The full working, with every formula, is in the 22-tab economics spreadsheet behind this brief.
